From one hub to a nation

Big ideas fail when they try to start big. Evolve_X starts with one chapter that has to work on its own, then copies what works.

Phases

Each phase begins only when the one before it has hit its targets.

  1. Prove the blueprint. Refine this plan in public. Form the cooperative under Texas law with experienced cooperative counsel. Recruit a founding board. Sign letters of intent with 50 merchants and 10 business clients, and line up a community lender.
  2. First chapter in Dallas–Fort Worth, years one and two. Reach 1,000 founding members. Launch the advocate for bills, claims and taxes, plus three business services. Train the first apprentice cohort. Publish the first patronage report, even if the number is small.
  3. A Texas network, years two to four. Open chapters in Amarillo, Austin, Houston and San Antonio. Hubs start earning from grid flexibility. The member-merchant network crosses the state, the creators’ registry opens, and a retiring owner’s business converts to member ownership for the first time.
  4. A national federation, years four to seven. Package the chapter-in-a-box playbook. Share models and software across chapters. Open a registered community offering so members can fund growth directly. Aim for 100 chapters.
  5. The commons dividend, ongoing. Work with legislators on an AI Commons Fund, using the co-op’s published track record as evidence that shared ownership of AI works.

How it scales in practice

  • The same chapter in every town. One open-source software stack, one hub design and one set of governance documents, adapted to local needs.
  • Shared where it counts. The federation handles models, security, compliance and bulk purchasing in exchange for a small share of each chapter’s revenue.
  • Software before buildings. A chapter can serve members before it owns a single server, renting capacity until owning it is cheaper.
  • Capital from many small sources. Member shares, retained patronage, community development lenders, mission-driven foundations and, later, registered community offerings. Large investors can lend. They can never control.
  • Growth from use, not hype. A chapter opens when members and merchants have signed up in advance, not when a market feels hot.

What could go wrong

Naming the risks is part of the plan.

RiskWhat we would do
Too few members joinStart with services people already pay for, and open chapters only after advance commitments.
Big tech gives similar tools awayCompete on trust, local service and ownership. Free tools don’t pay you back or give you a vote.
An AI mistake hurts a memberA person reviews high-stakes decisions, the co-op carries insurance, and incidents are reported publicly.
Computing costs swingRent before buying, buy as a federation, and sell spare capacity.
The rules changeStay inside existing law: cooperative statutes, consumer protection and securities law, with counsel reviewing every new program.
Leaders capture it, or it driftsThe mission trustee, term limits, worker board seats, open books and the annual drift audit.

What we will publish

Every quarter, for every chapter.

  • Members and chapters.
  • Median monthly savings per member.
  • Patronage returned, in cash and to ownership accounts.
  • The wage floor and the pay ratio.
  • Claims and appeals won for members.
  • Findings from the drift audit, and what was done about them.
