The eight pillars

Each pillar is a service people would want even if they didn’t own it. Ownership is what makes it fair. Each one starts small, with a first version a single chapter can run, and has a clear path to scale.

Own

The income from AI stays with the people who use it.

What it does
The co-op owns its computing in community hubs instead of renting everything from a distant cloud, and runs its own AI models for members and local businesses. Over time it also helps retiring small-business owners sell to their employees and community, then upgrades those businesses with AI so they stay competitive.
First version
One Dallas–Fort Worth hub in a repurposed commercial building, with a modest server cluster running open models for the co-op’s own services. Capacity is rented until owning it is clearly cheaper.
How it scales
Each new chapter builds a hub from the same design. The federation buys chips and power together and shifts work between hubs as demand and electricity prices change.
Grew from
Rousix Mining Machines, the “data center in a box,” and the enterprise acquisition strategy. Kept: people-owned infrastructure and owning productive businesses. Changed: no hardware sold as an income product, and friendly local buyouts instead of takeovers.

Work

Good jobs in the AI economy, open to everyone.

What it does
Every worker earns at least a living wage, the highest pay is capped at five times the lowest, and both numbers are published. Paid apprenticeships train members as hub technicians, AI supervisors and service specialists, the people who check the machines’ work.
First version
A paid cohort of 20 apprentices recruited from members, including people returning to work after incarceration or long unemployment.
How it scales
Graduates staff the next chapters. A shared curriculum and credential travel with them from town to town.
Grew from
The labor-anchored economic framework and research on wage integrity. Kept: labor should share in the value it creates. Changed: a published wage floor and pay ratio replace the 80/20 revenue formula, because a co-op can actually keep that promise.

Share

Your spending builds ownership instead of disappearing.

What it does
Local businesses join as member merchants and accept instant pay-by-bank payments instead of cards. They keep most of the 2 to 3 percent a card network would have charged, and share part of that saving with the customer as patronage credit toward their ownership account.
First version
Fifty member merchants in one part of Dallas, a simple pay-by-bank checkout, and a public monthly report of how much was returned to members.
How it scales
The merchant network spreads city by city on standard bank payment rails. No new currency is needed.
Grew from
Consumer recapitalization, the liquidity pools, and the Intermetanet’s universal point of sale. Kept: everyday purchases should build equity. Changed: funded by real savings on card fees, not by a premium added to what you buy.

Protect

Someone finally on your side of the claim.

What it does
An AI advocate reads your bills, insurance denials and contracts, spots errors, and drafts appeals, with trained staff reviewing anything important. Later, members can pool risk in a member-owned mutual that publishes how fast and how fairly it pays claims.
First version
Medical-bill and insurance-denial appeals for members, with every outcome counted and reported.
How it scales
Add auto, home and small-business claims. Partner with, or form, a licensed mutual once there is enough data and capital to do it responsibly.
Grew from
The insurance system designed to end “delay, deny, defend,” and research on agent-based claims processing. Kept: the mission. Changed: start as the policyholder’s advocate before trying to become an insurer.

Settle

Money that is clear, on time, and never a surprise.

What it does
Your advocate tracks income from every source, sets aside what you’ll owe in taxes, files with you, and explains everything in plain English. Every co-op agreement has a plain-language version, and that version is the one that counts.
First version
Tax set-aside and filing help for gig workers and small businesses, plus plain-language member agreements.
How it scales
Connects to payroll, banks and brokerages through standard links. Plain-language contract templates are published openly for any business to use.
Grew from
AIRSP, GTMS and TexasSwap. Kept: automatic tax settlement and contracts in plain English. Changed: works with the tax system as it is, and drops the exchange and its token.

Create

Credit and payment when AI learns from your work.

What it does
A creators’ registry where musicians, artists and writers record their work, set their terms for AI training, and get paid through collective licensing. Content credentials travel with the work so attribution survives.
First version
A Texas music and visual-arts registry and one licensing agreement with an AI developer that pays registered creators.
How it scales
Grows into a creator-owned collective-licensing body for AI training rights, alongside existing rights organizations.
Grew from
UAIE and the music and film royalty plans. Kept: automatic attribution and royalties. Changed: a registry and enforceable contracts instead of a token layer.

Learn

Learning that pays off, owned by the people who teach.

What it does
An open library of lessons owned and governed by educators, AI-literacy courses for members of every age, and credentials that lead directly to apprenticeships and jobs.
First version
Free evening courses at the hub on using AI safely at work and at home, and a teacher-run pilot for sharing lessons.
How it scales
Teachers who contribute share in licensing income, and credentials are recognized across every chapter.
Grew from
Teacher’s Partner and the incentive-based learning programs. Kept: educators in control, and learning that leads to income. Changed: the commons belongs to the teachers who build it.

Home

Keep families housed and keep wealth in the neighborhood.

What it does
Early help for families falling behind on a mortgage: modification requests, equity-preserving sales, and referrals to HUD-approved housing counselors. Bank-owned homes are offered first to owner-occupants, tenants and community land trusts.
First version
A foreclosure help line run through the advocate with partner counselors, and one partnership with a community land trust.
How it scales
A community acquisition fund buys bank-owned homes and returns them to resident ownership, city by city.
Grew from
MREO, Myers Real Estate Owned. Kept: preserving families’ equity and moving distressed property efficiently. Changed: residents and land trusts come before outside buyers.

Underneath every pillar

Identity you control

Members prove who they are with digital credentials kept on their own phone. The co-op keeps only what the law requires, never builds a biometric database, and never sells member data. Members can opt into an opportunity map that matches their skills to work and learning.

Grew from Biometric ID and the Living Census.

Second chances

A criminal record doesn’t bar anyone from membership, and it only matters for a job where the law requires it. People who have paid their debt deserve a way back into the economy, and a stake in it.

Grew from the redemption stories at the heart of the original project.

Community hubs

Each hub is a public-facing place: a workspace, a classroom, and home to the co-op’s computers. Membership is sliding-scale, so the people who need it most can use it.

Grew from the economic hub plan for a restored Plano office campus.
